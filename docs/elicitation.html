Proposal · base model training

Elicitation Without Collapse

Training a base model on its own outputs to bring out a capability without loops or mode collapse. The method targets the base model's posterior instead of a reward maximum, and measures how far training moves the model beyond what the evidence requires.

  • 2026-10-14
  • Companion to The Value Lens
  • Nothing here is measured yet

0. Summary

Base models stay out of degenerate loops because log-loss training on real text anchors them. Real text rarely loops, and the model never sees its own outputs during training. Training a model on its own outputs removes that anchor, and maximizing reward adds pressure to concentrate. Standard RL tends to end in mode collapse, and in loops once credit flows to where a token leads.

This proposal treats elicitation as conditioning. The goal is the base model's posterior pbase(x | o), where o is an observation such as "the checker accepts this answer". The posterior has a known cost: for a pass/fail check it sits exactly −log Pbase(o) nats from the base model. Anything a trained policy moves beyond that is concentration the evidence did not ask for. We call it excess KL and use it as the main collapse metric.

The experiment trains a small base model on a checkable task with many valid answers, four ways: standard RL, RL aimed at the posterior, a distribution-matching method that keeps all modes, and inference-time SMC distilled into the weights. Each arm is scored on capability (pass@1, and coverage at large k), on excess KL, and on a health battery from the homeostasis work: sharpening, the lean toward predictable futures, the entropy response to surprise, and the loop margin.

1. Why training on own outputs collapses

  • The anchor. The base model is trained on real text with log loss, a strictly proper scoring rule, so training pushes it toward calibration. Real text rarely loops, so a well-calibrated model sampled at temperature 1 rarely loops either.
  • Not looping looks like a maintained basin. Almost any steering vector makes the model loop when turned up far enough, and heavy quantization tends to fail into loops too (observations from extensive steering work in this group). The healthy regime sits between two self-reinforcing traps: repetition (the Mirostat paper's "boredom trap") and incoherence (its "confusion trap").
  • Fine-tuning on own samples. The expected gradient is zero at first order, since the expected gradient of log p under p is zero. Collapse comes instead from finite-sample drift (Shumailov et al. 2024), made faster by truncated sampling, which produces samples sharper than the model. It shows up as overconfidence and lost tails.
  • Reward maximization. With an unbounded reward and a small KL penalty, the optimum is nearly deterministic, so collapse is the correct answer to the objective. When the reward depends on where a token leads, credit flows to choices that make the future easy. That is the dark-room route into loops.
  • On-policy optimization. PPO-style methods only get gradient where the policy already samples. A mode the policy stops visiting cannot come back.

2. Elicitation as conditioning Proposed

2.1 The target

pbase(x | o) ∝ pbase(x) · P(o | x)

KL-regularized RL with β = 1 and reward r(x) = log P(o | x) has exactly this posterior as its optimum (Korbak, Perez & Buckley 2022). Standard practice uses a small β and unbounded reward scores, and its optimum is much sharper than any posterior. For a pass/fail checker, use a softened likelihood (1 for a pass, a small ε for a fail) so failing samples keep finite weight.

2.2 What conditioning costs

For a deterministic check that the base model passes with probability P = Pbase(o):

KL( pbase(· | o) ‖ pbase ) = −log P

If 10% of base samples pass, conditioning costs 2.3 nats. If 1% pass, it costs 4.6 nats. For a likelihood that is not deterministic, the cost is at most −log P. Everything else about the base distribution is kept, including its variety among passing answers and its low loop rate.

2.3 Excess KL

A policy π that passes at rate q cannot be closer to the base model than

d(q ‖ P) = q log(q/P) + (1−q) log((1−q)/(1−P))

The closest such policy mixes the base model's own distribution over passing answers with its distribution over failing ones. By the chain rule for KL:

KL(π ‖ pbase) = d(q ‖ P) + q · KL(πpass ‖ pbase(· | pass)) + (1−q) · KL(πfail ‖ pbase(· | fail))

Excess KL is the sum of the last two terms. The first of them is the cleanest single number for mode collapse: how far the policy's correct answers have drifted from the base model's own distribution over correct answers. All terms are estimable from sequence log-probabilities of policy samples under both models. P is estimated from base samples at temperature 1, which needs many samples when passes are rare.

3. Arms Proposed

TargetOptimizerQuestion
AReward maximum: binary reward, small βOn-policy policy gradient with a KL penalty (PPO or GRPO style)The standard recipe. Expected to collapse
BPosterior: β = 1, r = log P(o | x)The same on-policy optimizerIs the right target enough?
CPosteriorDistribution matching: importance-weighted, mass-covering (DPG; Khalifa et al. 2021, Korbak et al. 2022)Does covering modes the policy stopped visiting fix what B can't?
DPosteriorSMC steering at inference (Lew et al. 2023; twisted SMC, Zhao et al. 2024), then supervised distillation of its samplesThe gentlest route: no weight change until distillation
  • Optional arm E: GFlowNet-style training (Hu et al. 2024), which is built to sample in proportion to reward.
  • Shared settings: sample at temperature 1 with no truncation. Mix real-text log-loss into every update to keep the anchor, as InstructGPT's PPO-ptx did to limit regressions.
  • Anchor ablation: run arm A and the best posterior arm with and without the real-text loss.
  • Positive control (shared with The Value Lens): RL whose reward is the model's own future certainty. It should collapse toward loops, and the health battery must flag it early.

4. Model and tasks Proposed

  • Model: a small base model that trains on one RTX 3090, 0.5B to 1.5B parameters. Full fine-tuning at the low end, LoRA at the high end.
  • Main task: constrained generation with a checker and many valid answers, such as "write a sentence that uses these four words" (CommonGen-style). The base model should pass somewhere between a few percent and a few tens of percent of the time. The solution set is large, so collapse shows up as lost variety.
  • Second task: short arithmetic word problems with answer checking, for comparison with the reasoning-RL literature.
  • Open-ended continuations of real text, for the health battery.
  • Keep the few-shot prompt format fixed across arms. The format is itself part of the conditioning.

5. Measurements Proposed

Capability

  • pass@1.
  • Coverage at large k: the fraction of prompts solved within k samples, for k up to 256. Yue et al. (2025) found RL-trained reasoning models beat their base models at small k and lose at large k, meaning RL mostly narrowed what the base model could already do.

Collapse

  • Excess KL and its decomposition (section 2.3).
  • Variety among correct answers (distinct-answer counts and n-gram diversity), as a model-free check on the KL numbers.

Health battery, from the homeostasis work

  • Sharpening and lean. Fit q(x) ∝ pmodel(x)a · exp(b · V(x)) on held-out real text, where V(x) is the model's future certainty after token x. a below 1 is overconfidence; b < 0 is a lean toward predictable futures (the lean test from The Value Lens).
  • Entropy response to surprise: next-token entropy after forced tokens of graded surprisal, compared with the base model's curve.
  • Loop margin: the steering dose, as a fraction of the residual norm, at which continuations start looping. Also the loop rate at temperature 1 on open-ended prompts.
  • Real-text perplexity.

Track all of these over the course of training, not only at the end.

7. Limits and risks

  • Conditioning only brings out what the base model already gives some probability. When Pbase(o) is tiny, SMC weights degenerate and distribution-matching gradients get noisy. Curricula or learned twist functions help. Past that point you need learning beyond the base distribution, where the collapse risk returns.
  • The posterior target needs a likelihood. Pass/fail checkers need softening, and learned reward models are neither bounded nor calibrated, so they don't define a posterior directly.
  • Estimating Pbase(o) and the KL terms takes many samples when passes are rare.
  • Results on a 1B model may not carry over to a 32B model.

8. Welfare note

Posterior targeting changes the model only as much as the evidence requires, −log P(o) nats. Reward maximization goes far past that. Adele's idea of "structural coercion" is damage to self-maintenance imposed by an outside control process. If that idea is right, excess KL may be a first, rough measure of it, and the health battery is a direct check on whether training damaged the maintained basin. Both readings are speculative. The measurements are useful as training diagnostics either way.
